### Introduction

Chapter 2Building a BusinessLesson 1The Virtual Coaching BusinessIntroductionBuilding a successful business requires time, perseverance, hard work, and patience, but the rewards are limitless! When the fitness professional is able to build a business, they create an opportunity to provide a life and the freedom they desire. Building a business requires a different skill set from simply performing a job. It often requires a wide range of personal, technical, and financial skills as well as entrepreneurial spirit.

One key step to building a successful business is developing a carefully crafted business plan. A business plan can help provide a blueprint and a structured plan that will help the fitness professional build a successful, thriving business.
Have a nice day Photo/Shutterstock.comBusiness plans will be specific to each business and business owner as business owners will have many different goals—both personal and professional. Developing a living business plan that is carefully thought out, well-crafted, and routinely updated will allow the fitness professional to build a business that fits their vision for both personal and professional success.

### Purpose of a Virtual Coaching Business

Chapter 2Building a BusinessLesson 1The Virtual Coaching BusinessPurpose of a Virtual Coaching BusinessOnline businesses have been around for over 30 years and businesses of all sizes now operate solely online. Businesses that were once only found in brick-and-mortar locations are now becoming more popular in an online marketplace rather than in physical locations. Groceries, shoes, and even new film releases are all presented to consumers from the comfort of their home. The world has shifted to an acceptance of and reliance on virtual commerce.
In the current market, there are many unknowns that turn into high risks for a new business. The risk of an economic downturn is always on the mind of a successful entrepreneur. When sales slow down, a business still has employees and bills to pay. Building a successful business that is dependent on someone else (e.g., training clients at a gym someone else owns) does not provide the entrepreneur with complete control over their success. Similarly, building a business with large overhead not only requires massive amounts of startup capital, but also places the fitness professional under financial stress during periods of slow economic growth.
Food for ThoughtIn 2019, the virtual fitness coaching industry was valued at over $6 billion and is expected to reach almost $60 billion by 2027 (Rake & Gaikwad, 2020).
dmitriy-orlovskiy/Shutterstock.comVirtual coaching businesses have existed in various forms for many years but have recently seen substantial growth as more consumers are becoming comfortable with, and even preferring, online coaching. This industry is at a tipping point that is starting to disrupt the in-person training industry, which has dominated the personal training market since its inception. Fitness professionals are becoming more comfortable with virtual coaching, which provides more opportunities for them to start their own business. Virtual coaches can start with little overhead and still work as an in-person fitness professional while building up their virtual coaching startup to a self-sustaining, thriving online business.

As the world shifts to an ever-larger virtual presence, clients are looking for ways to integrate health and fitness into the virtual world. Fitness professionals must find a path to meet the demand for virtual services that not only provides the best training for clients, but also provides the same, or greater, job stability that in-person training offers.

### Qualities of an Entrepreneur

Chapter 2Building a BusinessLesson 1The Virtual Coaching BusinessQualities of an EntrepreneurThe Internet has made being an entrepreneur or business owner more accessible. The terms entrepreneur and business owner are often used interchangeably. However, there are differences between the two (Marks, 2012; Spring, 2014). In fact, over 18% of small businesses do not see themselves as entrepreneurs (Huefner & Hunt, 1998).
Small business owners are typically local businesses with one or a few physical locations within a small geographic area. They are often well-known throughout the community as a business owner and have deep personal connections with their customers. A business owner typically spends most of their time working in their business instead of on their business. Entrepreneurs are often small business owners as well; however, sometimes entrepreneurs may enter a new business but not own it. Other times, an entrepreneur may be in between businesses after selling theirs.
Food for ThoughtWorking in the business includes training clients, responding to customer service e-mails, and bookkeeping.

Working on the business includes developing training protocols, creating and testing marketing strategies, crafting customer service policies, structuring corporate guidelines, and building financial projections.

In the early stages of a business, the business owner will be focused primarily on working in the business. However, as a business grows, the entrepreneur will need to shift much of their focus to working on the business.
EntrepreneurA business owner who develops systematic, repeatable, scalable, and teachable processes to grow a business.
Business ownerAn individual or group who owns a business entity and has final decision-making authority as well as first right to profits

Executive Fitness Example1. Who is the target audience? What challenge does the target audience face that the business can help solve?

The target audience is busy business professionals who want to give their health, fitness, and nutrition a higher priority but have difficulty finding a sustainable path to achieve their goals.
2. What is the fitness professional’s personal connection and motivation to help solve this problem?

This business is owned by someone who, prior to becoming a fitness professional, worked a corporate office job. Firsthand experience with the lack of personal health goals in accomplished individuals is vast. Balancing a 40+-hour work week with family life, being sedentary, lacking motivation to exercise, and not understanding there is a sustainable path to health are all common realities. This allows for empathy with clients and for developing a plan that will fit their aspirations.
3. What does the business look like in 1, 5, and 10 years?

During the first year, systems will be developed and refined while the company services the professional market the owner is most familiar with—those working larger corporate jobs in upper to middle management. The company will also start to bring on employees at that time to ensure high quality of service is maintained. At the 5-year mark, the business will have started partnering with corporations to provide in-house wellness services to employees, as well as continuing to grow the direct-to-client coaching services. At the 10-year mark, the business will be a large company in the online training industry, generating millions of dollars of revenue annually. This growth will require an entire professional management team, along with a strong corporate structure and a constantly updated and refined business plan.

### Company Description

Chapter 2Building a BusinessLesson 2Building a Virtual Coaching Business Plan: Part ICompany DescriptionA description of the company should be concise yet detailed. Simply stating that a business will conduct online personal training is too vague. Conversely, going into detail about what estimated customer acquisition cost (CAC) will be is too in-depth for the executive summary (this will be included in expanded sections of the business plan such as marketing or financial). A description of the business should discuss the purpose of the business and what makes it unique. The uniqueness is often described as the unique selling proposition (USP). This is the place that the fitness professional should explain why customers will choose their business over a competitor.
Highlight the true uniqueness of the business. Mentioning features of a business are simply not enough. There was a time when responding to an e-mail within 24 hours or offering text/messaging to a client was unique and rare. However, now that there are more online coaching businesses, these features have become the standard. The fitness professional needs a USP that only they bring to their clients.
niroworld/Shutterstock.comIt is also a good idea, and common practice, to include information on any of the company founders, along with their backgrounds. Even if the business owner is a solopreneur, they should be able to sell themselves and explain why their background and experience makes them the most suited to bring their USP to the market.
The company description should also include the management structure of the company. If the business will be starting with partners or employees, their roles and responsibilities should be clearly defined here. Highlight why these individuals will be vital to the business’s initial success. If these employees are not included in the description and backgrounds of the company founders, then they should be included here.
Most entrepreneurs are solopreneurs at first. They are the only employee in the business. This means they are working both in and on the business. Take this opportunity to clarify what roles everyone involved will be taking on and what roles may be contracted out. For instance, most entrepreneurs will take on marketing, but few will do their own taxes.
Helpful HintThere are many gig services, companies that connect consumers or businesses with freelancers to perform various tasks or jobs, that can be found online for minimal cost. These can be utilized for things like creating infographics, editing blogs, and editing videos.

It is also possible to find reputable and affordable online services for things like accounting and legal advice.
Customer acquisition cost (CAC)How much a new customer costs a business – typically, the cost is determined through money spent on advertising.
Unique selling proposition (USP)The unique benefit or feature of a product or service that differentiates a business from other businesses offering a similar service or product.

### Identify Target Audience

Chapter 2Building a BusinessLesson 2Building a Virtual Coaching Business Plan: Part IIdentify Target AudienceThe final section of the Executive Summary in the business plan should identify who the clients will be. Most business owners already have some idea of who their target audience is. Having too wide a target audience, the business’s message will get lost and not resonate with clients. A narrow target audience and will be too specific for anyone to hear the message. The target audience should be someone the fitness professional can relate to, that they know their USP will help, and that they feel will provide the business with enough clients to grow and expand.

Realize that every business will have clients who are outside of their target audience and their target audience will change over time, especially in the early stages of the business. For example, some fitness professionals may start out only wanting to work with competitive physique athletes, but quickly find out they prefer working with seniors who are trying to improve their quality of life.

The ideal client’s goal is not just to lose weight or get into shape. The ideal client’s goal is to find a balance between life, work, and physical health.

In order to provide prompt service to clients and properly manage the limited time a small business owner or business professional has, the ideal client is located in North America. Exact geographical location outside of that does not matter, as this is a virtual coaching company.

### Products and Services

Chapter 2Building a BusinessLesson 2Building a Virtual Coaching Business Plan: Part IProducts and ServicesOnline Personal Training is not a product per se, but rather a service. The product description in the business plan must include detailed descriptions of what features will be brought to the market and what makes the product or service unique. Products and/or services should always be improving and changing. Offering the same service with no improvements is a quick way to become irrelevant in the highly competitive landscape of businesses, especially with online businesses. With that said, the downfall of many entrepreneurs is trying to perfect their product too much prior to actually having real-world feedback.
This section of the business plan should focus on the minimum viable product (MVP) that the business will be starting with. Remember the business plan is a living document that can be modified and updated as product offerings change. There are many approaches to developing an MVP. In this section, it will be broken down step-by-step to develop multiple product offerings that can get to market quickly and are easy to improve based on feedback from customers.
Helpful HintBefore starting to develop the MVP, do some simple market research and gain insight to what future competitors are doing. If the top competitors of the business are known, browse those companies’ websites, e-mail the companies with questions, and read their content. If the business’s competitors are unknown, search online for keywords that potential clients would most likely use to find services the business is going to offer. This is not to steal anything from competitors but, rather, to ensure the business is offering industry-standard items. The fitness professional may even find that what all competitor companies offer is not good enough for their clients and they will have the opportunity to build an industry-changing product.
Minimum viable product (MVP)A product that provides the bare minimum features to satisfy customer demand – this is the foundation for a product that will be developed and modified based on market feedback.

### Canned, Predesigned Programs

Chapter 2Building a BusinessLesson 2Building a Virtual Coaching Business Plan: Part ICanned, Predesigned ProgramsMany online personal trainers seek to write predesigned exercise and nutrition programs and sell them as one-off purchases. This approach may not be the most sustainable path for all businesses to take. Just like working with an in-person training client, training programs should be customized for each person. A training program that works for a 28-year-old former college athlete who wants to focus on muscle hypertrophy most likely will not work for a 65-year-old who wants to improve their ability to carry grocery bags up the stairs.
Figure Customize Predesigned Training TemplatesConsider the customer lifetime value (CLV). A customer who makes a recurring monthly payment for customized training at $100 per month for 4 months is worth 16 times more than someone who pays $25 for a one-time exercise program. They are also more likely to refer clients back to the business versus the templated training program that can be e-mailed to a friend.

This is not to say templates do not have a place in business. Templates can be massive time savers for the busy fitness professional and can make processes and work more efficient and consistent. Having pre-made training templates that are easy for the business owner to modify and customize for each client will save hours of work developing programming for each client (Figure: Customize Predesigned Training Templates). While similar, this differs from using a generic approach to programming for every client. For instance, developing five training templates with no specific exercises can save time having to recreate the same thing for every client.
Here are some template examples:


    Template 1: A 2-day-per-week full-body program
    Template 2: A 3-day-per-week program broken down into push/pull/legs
    Template 3: A 5-day-per-week program consisting of back/biceps, shoulders/chest/triceps, and legs

Lastly, consider the customer acquisition cost (CAC). New customers are not free—they cost time, money, and energy. The business may not have a marketing budget, but the time that is spent making sales and creating content is time away from running the business and time away from providing services to clients. It is generally more cost effective to sell to someone who is already a customer than to try and acquire a new customer.

### Pricing Strategies

Chapter 2Building a BusinessLesson 2Building a Virtual Coaching Business Plan: Part IPricing StrategiesThe following are four different pricing strategies that will help guide the entrepreneur pricing strategy in a simple way:
Economy Pricing: Low priced, affordable pricing for everyone – Profit margins in this pricing category are small. This pricing strategy is designed to target a mass, broad market. In a service industry, this pricing strategy is typically reserved for large corporations.

Skimming Pricing: This pricing strategy charges a higher price at the start of the company and eventually lowers the pricing as more competitors enter the market. This strategy is typically used for companies entering a new market or market segment that is not very competitive.

Penetration Pricing: This strategy sets pricing lower at the start of the company in order to gain a share of the market quickly. Once the business has met its market share goals, prices are raised to a higher level. This strategy also allows for feedback from customers and helps with the decision on where a valid price point should be set.

Premium Pricing: Setting a high price is used to define the product or brand as a luxury item. In the mind of the consumer, the high price must mean a high-quality product. This strategy is used by high-end brands in the automotive and luxury watch industries.

### Pricing Strategies

Chapter 2Building a BusinessLesson 2Building a Virtual Coaching Business Plan: Part IPricing StrategiesThe following are four different pricing strategies that will help guide the entrepreneur pricing strategy in a simple way:
Economy Pricing: Low priced, affordable pricing for everyone – Profit margins in this pricing category are small. This pricing strategy is designed to target a mass, broad market. In a service industry, this pricing strategy is typically reserved for large corporations.

Skimming Pricing: This pricing strategy charges a higher price at the start of the company and eventually lowers the pricing as more competitors enter the market. This strategy is typically used for companies entering a new market or market segment that is not very competitive.

Penetration Pricing: This strategy sets pricing lower at the start of the company in order to gain a share of the market quickly. Once the business has met its market share goals, prices are raised to a higher level. This strategy also allows for feedback from customers and helps with the decision on where a valid price point should be set.

Premium Pricing: Setting a high price is used to define the product or brand as a luxury item. In the mind of the consumer, the high price must mean a high-quality product. This strategy is used by high-end brands in the automotive and luxury watch industries.
