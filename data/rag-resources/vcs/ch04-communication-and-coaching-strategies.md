### Introduction

Chapter 4Communication and Coaching StrategiesLesson 1Virtual Coaching 101IntroductionCommunication plays a tremendous role in the relationship fitness professionals have with their clients. With in-person training, fitness professionals often rely on a combination of non-verbal and verbal communication strategies to relay instructions, cues, incentives, and enthusiasm. However, there has been a shift in fitness service offerings due to advances in technology and alterations in lifestyle factors. In-person training environments are now also offered in hybrid or fully virtual, online training formats. Therefore, fitness professionals should be familiar with various forms of communication and learn how to tailor different technologies and methods of communicating to achieve greater success for their clients.
Online platforms have opened more environments and increased opportunities to cultivate client relationships to help clients reach their goals. The fitness professional should work at not only developing the technical skills to navigate online training, but also how to interview, build rapport, and set goals with clients. While there are many common elements between in-person and online training, there are distinct differences as well—requiring fitness professionals who train clients virtually to place greater focus on developing skills to combat distraction and maintain excellent client connection.
This chapter will address core principles of virtual communications. The fitness professional will be able to use this information to guide interactions with clients from consultation to regular check-ins. While the technical aspects of training, like data collection and sheet creation, are important to working and tracking progress with clients, ultimately, it all comes down to the relationship the fitness professional is able to build and maintain with the client.

### Communication Platforms

Chapter 4Communication and Coaching StrategiesLesson 1Virtual Coaching 101Communication PlatformsThe method the fitness professional uses to connect with a client will depend on the technical aspects of their online setup and if they work for themselves or with a company. There are a variety of communication platforms the fitness professional can use; in fact, it is not uncommon to use more than one to service clients. It is important to understand how each platform works in order to assess which one will work best for the fitness professional and their clients depending on the situation and program needs.
For example, one type of communication platform is a mobile app. NASM’s EDGE app allows fitness professionals to access client information, use and perform assessments, and tailor workouts all from one platform interface. While this app services a certain set of needs and requirements, each fitness professional will want to determine the right system and communication platform from which to deliver their programs and services (Table: Communication Platforms).
Communication Platforms
Email is the most common application for communicating with online clients. In 2018, over 281 billion emails were sent and received each day (Clement, 2019). It is also the most personal form of virtual communication and often easy to use. In its most simple use, an online fitness professional needs only an email address to start working with clients virtually. Using email for client communication comes with many benefits, including accessibility. For instance, when people go online, they often start with e-mail.

Email also decreases the chance of technology fumbles and errors or the need for third-party communication systems. Email provides a clear one-on-one platform for talking between clients and fitness professionals and builds a foundation for confidentiality and trust in the relationship.

Online communities or apps can include private company memberships or social media platforms like Facebook. With private company membership, a fitness professional can have their own membership site or app where individuals can access their information and update and share results with their fitness professional and often other members. With certain apps and membership groups, fitness professionals have the capability of delivering programs, exercise videos, and nutritional information and to track their client’s data while also allowing the client to see and track the data themselves.

With social media supported groups like Facebook, individuals can receive support, links, and updates just as with the private community; however, the fitness professional and company leader do not control the design or updates of the space. There are also few ways to track data or performance. Fitness professionals may be limited based on what they want to execute and how much they can achieve. There is also an increased possibility of distraction or disruption in goals due to the other elements that exist with and comprise social media. That being said, the collective community provided with social media can be inspiring and unifying.

Text messaging (texting) is a form of communicating with the client directly via the messaging application on mobile devices, which essentially bypasses e-mail. It gives direct written messages to the client, usually instantly. Fitness professionals can use texting to provide or receive immediate answers to questions or for reminding clients of goals they need to achieve for the day. Reminders are a particularly interesting point of focus for fitness professionals as some studies show that texting or mobile app messaging may help increase attention toward health goals (Schwebel & Larimer, 2018).

A growing technology trend in client communication is video calls and video conferencing between fitness professionals and clients. Smartphone applications like FaceTime or WhatsApp or online conferencing services like Google Hangouts or Zoom means fitness professionals can interact with and watch their clients perform workouts and then relay comments or advice based on body language and non-verbal cues. Moreover, fitness professionals can visually demonstrate, just as they can in-person, how to perform exercises and movements correctly.

These applications will often have recording options, which can allow the fitness professional to replay calls and take more time to analyze and dissect assessments. Furthermore, fitness professionals can use these videos as “before” examples to measure and track client results against, allowing them to show progress to the client as time goes on.

The phone is still a solid standby for quick, easy, and instantaneous communication. While not all client populations may have access or the technological ability to use video or application services, a large majority do have telephone access. Fitness professionals and clients can communicate their needs and instructions and provide instant responses.

### Synchronous and Asynchronous Communication

Chapter 4Communication and Coaching StrategiesLesson 1Virtual Coaching 101Synchronous and Asynchronous CommunicationFitness professionals should consider which environment and type of communication is the right fit for their clients. Specifically, what form of and how much synchronous and/or asynchronous communication will be most effective for the fitness professional and client relationship, as well as intended goals and outcomes of the program.
Synchronous communication occurs at the same time (or of the moment) between more than one individual. An example of virtual synchronous communication would be a live video training session with a fitness professional (Figure: Synchronous Communication Example). The client, in this instance, would be receiving live (or of the moment) cues about how to move or adjust their form or rep counts and how to best proceed to the next exercise.
FigureSynchronous Communication ExampleSynchronous communication is closest to the traditional personal training environment. If live video streams are used between a client and their fitness professional, many of the same benefits of in-person training can exist. Fitness professionals can comment immediately and directly and clients can pose questions about their program in real-time. Clients can also see the body language and physical responses of their fitness professional. Smiling, eye contact, and gestures can all relay enthusiasm and encouragement for positive reinforcement. Communication platforms that are synchronous include video calls and video conferencing and the phone.
Asynchronous communication is communication that occurs on delay (or at a later time) between more than one individual. E-mail is one form of asynchronous communication. For example, a fitness professional sends an instructional email to a client for that week’s exercise program and then the client responds back at a later time with questions or comments (Figure: Asynchronous Communication Example). Other communication platforms that are asynchronous include online communities, certain apps, and texting.

### When to Use Which Communication Method

Chapter 4Communication and Coaching StrategiesLesson 1Virtual Coaching 101When to Use Which Communication MethodBeyond understanding the concepts of asynchronous and synchronous communication is learning when to make the best use of each. Both forms of communications have advantages and disadvantages. The style of communication and when it is used is a skill as much as training itself. For example, too many reminders or interrupting messages from apps or online communities can end up creating a haze or ignoring effect (Kushlev et al., 2016). However, if a client needs time-sensitive assistance, they may benefit more from synchronous communication. For example, if a client needs multiple answers on how to handle a nutritional strategy problem, it might benefit both the fitness professional and client timewise to opt for a quick phone call and have a back-and-forth conversation.
Creating office hours and boundaries for synchronous communication is important for both the fitness professional and the client. Mobile app messaging, for example, can be a form of both asynchronous and synchronous communication, depending on the parameters. In some instances, the messaging is set during predetermined hours and a client can receive instant messaging or communication from the fitness professional. For example, if a client is in the middle of a training session and does not understand what level of fatigue in training they should achieve during their exercise sets, they can instant message their fitness professional and receive an immediate answer.
Other texting and messaging can be asynchronous, such as reminders or encouragements sent on schedules or not in real-time. Clients can find these messages useful, but a fitness professional needs to be careful to not constantly send reminders, updates, and messages to their client if the trigger effect is not needed. If a fitness professional does not respect the client’s boundaries, it could lead to the client consciously or subconsciously viewing the messages as ineffective or irrelevant. These clients would need less-frequent and more specifically targeted, asynchronous communication, such as email.

### Communicating Online: Asking Questions

Chapter 4Communication and Coaching StrategiesLesson 1Virtual Coaching 101Communicating Online: Asking QuestionsOnce the fitness professional determines which communication type and platforms they will be using, the next crucial phase is understanding how to communicate with potential clients. Assuming clients have gone through the qualifying process and ultimately signed on to work with a fitness professional or company, the next interactions with a client are the most important a fitness professional can have. The manner in which fitness professionals talk to clients, how they ask and respond to questions, and the plans they make for the future together pave the road for their relationship.
Collecting Client Information: Open-Ended and Closed-Ended QuestionsCommunicating virtually requires fitness professionals to effectively collect the information needed from the client to guide them in their journey while simultaneously using a client-centered approach and fostering personal autonomy. An effective way of achieving this is to allow the client to speak for themselves by using the right style of interviewing to evoke detailed and thoughtful responses. This style of interviewing revolves around open-ended questions.
Open-ended questions are questions worded in a manner that evokes thoughtfulness and reflection in the individual who is being asked. They cannot be answered by a simple “yes” or “no” response. These types of questions can help clients to explore deeper into their desires, goals, and emotional positions, while helping fitness professionals find out what motivates them and where their values lie and further understand their thought processes.
Most often, open-ended questions begin with “Why,” “What,” “How,” or “Tell me” and allow the client to speak freely. For example, “Why are you looking to start an exercise program at this time?
Using open-ended questions in interviews and interactions can help fitness professionals better understand their client’s motives, goals, and potential obstacles, which can help them find unique solutions to various circumstances. Often, this can lead to the client finding answers on their own by giving them the chance to voice their thoughts and connect different experiences together. Additionally, open-ended questions encourage the client to elaborate and give more nuanced explanations. At first, certain details may not seem relevant or important, but these can be the very details fitness professionals need most in order to tailor a program for success.
Open-ended questionsQuestions worded in a manner that evokes thoughtfulness and reflection in the individual who is being asked.

### Virtual Goal Setting

Chapter 4Communication and Coaching StrategiesLesson 2Virtual Accountability StrategiesVirtual Goal SettingOnce the fitness professional has asked open- and closed-ended questions in order to build rapport with the client, the next step is to set goals for their program using a framework or system like SMART goals. In the questionnaire or consultation process, a fitness professional should ask the client what their current short-term and long-term goals are, what they want to achieve, and by when they want to achieve it. From there, using SMART as a guideline, the fitness professional will have the ability to work with the client and determine the pace and appropriate method for achieving those goals.
SMART GoalsGoals are actions or results an individual wants to achieve, generally within a specified timeframe. Fitness professionals will encounter clients who have a variety of goals ranging from increasing strength, daily mobility, muscle growth, and cardiovascular improvements. Each client will have different goals and different timelines and expectations for their goals. One of the roles of a fitness professional is to be the manager and facilitator of their client’s goals. One way a fitness professional can do this is by following a researched and tested formula of goal setting called SMART goals. SMART is an acronym that stands for Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, and Time-bound. The concept of SMART originated in the 1950s in management and company improvement research (Day & Tosey, 2011). Since that time, SMART goals have been found useful as a framework for a wide variety of goal setting circumstances.
SMART Goals - Definition and ExamplesDefining a SMART Goal
Application
Specific

Is the goal specific?
The goal should not be vague or unclear. It should be targeted, purposeful, and specific in nature. The more narrowed the focus on the goal, the more likely the things needed to be done to achieve the goal will be as well.

Example of a non-specific goal: I want to exercise more.

Example of a specific goal: I will follow a strength building program and exercise 4 days every week.
Measurable

How will the goal be measured?
The goal should have metrics and references of measure. The fitness professional should be able to trend and study the progress of the client’s success toward their goal.

Example of non-measured goal: The client updates you every few weeks saying they have completed (and sometimes not completed) their goals with very little charting or records.

Example of a measured goal: Once a week, the client updates a premade form covering the exercise, reps, sets, and intensity required to gauge their program results and direct any changes.
Achievable

Is the goal one that can be completed?
The goal should be achievable. It should be technically possible to accomplish.

Example of non-achievable goal: A middle-aged client wants a training program to increase their height by 5 inches.

Example of an achievable goal: A middle-aged client wants a training program to help improve their standing posture.
Relevant

Is the goal realistically achievable for that person?

Is the goal personally meaningful?
While similar to an attainable goal, this goal needs to be relevant and realistically possible for the specific client. Simply because a goal is technically attainable does not mean the goal itself is realistic for that particular individual.

Example of non-realistic goal: A client wants to lose 20 lbs of pure fat in 1 month.

Example of an achievable goal: A client wants to lose 2 lbs of pure fat in 1 month.
Time-Bound

Is there a definite timeline for completion of the goal?
The goal should have a start date and a termination date. If multiple goals are being selected, each of them should move in unison with the other and have time-specific dates for reassessment.

Example of non-time-bound goal: A client wants to gain muscle at some point. 

Example of a time-bound goal: A client wants to gain 2 lbs of muscle in a 3-month time period.
SMART goalsAn acronym that stands for specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound goals.

### Outcome and Process Goals

Chapter 4Communication and Coaching StrategiesLesson 2Virtual Accountability StrategiesOutcome and Process GoalsAn outcome goal is defined as the target or definitive end point of the set goal. For example, if the goal is to gain 2 pounds of muscle by the client’s birthday, that is the outcome. Outcome goals are also synonymous with concepts like short-term and long-term goals.
A process goal is the method or alteration of habit used to achieve that goal. For example, to gain 2 pounds of muscle by their birthday, the client will need to take part in a nutrition and specific exercise training program to support that outcome.
In process goals, there can also be performance goals. Performance goals are defined by setting a new achievement(s) within the performance being measured. For example, if the process goal is to perform three sets of 10 deadlifts in the workout, the performance goal could be to track the ability to increase weight when doing a deadlift.
Generally speaking, time is typically best spent working on process goals. While it is important to have a larger focus on the end direction or outcome, the short-term process completion can help a client build momentum and self-efficacy toward achieving continued success with their goals. Research suggests that short-term goals can be defined as any goal attempted to be completed in less than 12 months (Ogbeiwi, 2017). Most literature agrees that 3 months is a good starting point for short-term goal achievement; however, individuals can focus efforts of smaller periods of time and interweave process goals alongside their outcome goals. For example, it can be more rewarding to an individual to set a process goal of training 4 days each week as they work toward achieving their 3-month goal. Each training session completed is another notch toward completion of the overall process and achievement of the goal.

### Measures of Accountability and Data Collection

Chapter 4Communication and Coaching StrategiesLesson 2Virtual Accountability StrategiesMeasures of Accountability and Data CollectionThe fitness professional and the client should come together on their ideal accountability parameters. There should be enough data collection to allow the fitness professional to have an educated understanding of the client’s progress toward their goals, but not so much information that the client is uncomfortable, it becomes unmanageable, or it is un-actionable. Autonomy in the check-in and data collection process is crucial to building a long-term relationship with the client. For example, some clients may find themselves negatively stimulated by daily weigh-ins while other clients find it motivating. This may also change through the course of working with the client and may need to be adjusted along the way. Clients should look forward to the weekly process of goal competition and data-collection. They should not have negative emotions attached to the process; otherwise, it is likely that compliance and dedication will wane.
Once the parameters have been established, a fitness professional needs to have some form of data collection available in order to track client progress. Fitness professionals can use applications in which the client inputs the data on their side and the fitness professional has visibility to see it, or they can receive the data from the client and input it themselves. Budget and technology capabilities will determine applications used. For example, Google Sheets™ is a spreadsheet application that is free with a Google account and allows both the fitness professional and the client to review, edit, and update the same document.
When a fitness professional has selected the best platform for communication and data collection and the client understands the purpose of each when working together, the next step is to actually start collecting data. In fact, data collection starts the moment a fitness professional begins interacting with a client. Every aspect of a fitness professional’s working relationship with clients involves important information that can be used to help clients achieve their goals. Fitness professionals learn most of this when initially evaluating and assessing their client; however, a certain amount of that information will need to be carried over from week-to-week in order to measure results and progress.
Physical DataPhysical data is comprised of an individual’s current makeup, such as age and anthropometric measurements like height, weight, body circumference, and body fat percentage. Depending on the client’s goals, these physical data measurements may be essential to tracking their progress toward physical goals.
Pictures can also be used to track physical data and for pre- or post-comparison photos. This is especially useful for clients who want to achieve changes in body composition, like fat loss or muscle gain, in which the scale or other measurements may not give the clearest or most accurate indication of progress toward the goal. However, remember, not all clients are comfortable sharing photos. It is incredibly important to provide a safe as well as secure space for clients to provide and store their progress photos.
Physical dataData comprised of an individual’s current makeup, such as age, height, weight, and measurements, including inches and body-fat percentage.

### Performance Data

Chapter 4Communication and Coaching StrategiesLesson 2Virtual Accountability StrategiesPerformance DataPerformance data includes alterations in any marker in which improvement or maintenance is looking to be achieved. Heart rate, for example, can be a great indicator of improved cardiovascular health in clients. Decreases in resting heart rate over time or alterations in heart rate variability (HRV) can be a sign of improved overall health (Kim et al., 2018). One-repetition maximum (1RM) testing (or other variants of repetition maximum testing), as well as some of the other objective assessments highlighted in the assessments chapter, are other reliable indicators of performance improvement (Levinger et al., 2009). By consistently collecting and tracking this data, even from a distance, fitness professionals can continue to monitor and track progress and guide their client in the pursuit and achievement of their goals.
Most performance data, as related to the previous examples, is fairly straightforward to collect, monitor, and track:


    Did the client lift heavier weights for a given exercise? Did they achieve more volume in their program?
    Has the client’s resting heart rate decreased over time? Has their HRV increased over time?
    Did their muscular endurance increase?
    Did they run farther, faster, or both?


These objective physical indicators of performance are generally easy to relay, read, and track over time.
Another performance parameter that can be used to measure progress is compliance ratings. Compliance ratings are how well a client felt they did in achieving that day’s process or goal. If a client has a goal of training 4 days a week, fitness professionals can provide them with a yes–no response section in their check-in. If they have achieved four “Yes” responses to the question “Did you train today?” then they have achieved a week of compliance.

Chapter 4Communication and Coaching StrategiesLesson 2Virtual Accountability StrategiesLifestyle Data Lifestyle data is made up of variables that can affect the success of outcome and process goals. ### Before the Check-In

Chapter 4Communication and Coaching StrategiesLesson 2Virtual Accountability StrategiesBefore the Check-InCommunication and organization are important elements for fitness professionals to maximize success in helping clients achieve their goals. As previously discussed, the rapport and manner in which a fitness professional speaks to their client is critical to fostering the relationship and building trust. On receipt of the first check-in, the fitness professional should already have a process and structure in place to keep clients’ data organized and easily accessible for review. Fitness professionals should always resist the urge or desire to put any of their own wishes, desires, or expectations onto the client.

### Communicating Online: Building Rapport

Chapter 4Communication and Coaching StrategiesLesson 2Virtual Accountability StrategiesCommunicating Online: Building Rapport Every interaction a fitness professional has with a client is a chance to strengthen or to weaken the relationship if they are not sensitive and thoughtful with their communication. To strengthen relationships, fitness professionals use rapport. Rapport is a relationship built on an agreement of mutual respect and understanding of the respective roles and empathy that allows for clear and effective communication. Building rapport is an ongoing process of active listening, shared experiences, and empathy. Rapport is practically impossible to build or establish under disingenuous circumstances. Choosing to care about clients is an investment worth putting time and effort into and can result in a gratifying career and an emotionally fulfilled client roster.
For success, patience and consideration is needed equally by both parties. Taking time to communicate with consideration and thought rather than rushing the process is likely to produce happy clients who feel looked after and valued. Alternatively, snappy, short, or truncated responses, can result in clients who become closed off, ashamed to ask questions, or feel as though they are not cared for. The right delivery can help assure that even challenging messages and more difficult conversations are perceived respectfully. Clients should feel trust in the professional and that they have their best intentions at heart.
For example, if a fitness professional encounters a situation where they are unable to respond immediately to a client because they need to carefully construct their thoughts, any potential tension can be mitigated by being honest and telling the truth (Figure: Thoughtful Response).

### Positive Affirmations

Chapter 4Communication and Coaching StrategiesLesson 2Virtual Accountability StrategiesPositive Affirmations Affirmations are another important piece of the fitness coaching communication toolbox. An affirmation is a positive statement highlighting traits, characteristics, actions, behaviors, etc.
Ultimately, the fitness professional wants clients to recognize these in themselves, but at the start of the relationship, it may require the fitness professional to highlight them. Fitness professionals can help clients reflect on themselves and see not just what they want to improve but also what they currently do well. In time, the objective is for clients to begin to adopt this positive mindset, observe their own victories, and begin to exhibit more confidence and self-compassion. When these personal skills grow, it can improve a client's ability to move forward with consistency rather than being anchored in place by negative thinking.
Additionally, fitness professionals should balance constructive feedback with positive affirmations as much as possible, and clients should hopefully leave each interaction feeling better than before and as though they are on the right track. Fitness professionals should empower the client while decreasing negativity. Improving exercise and nutrition habits are staples in individuals who embark on a journey toward self-improvement. That journey should always come from a positive place. For example, many individuals who seek out weight-loss programs who are met with stark opposition and shaming will actually gain weight (Sutin & Terracciano, 2013). Research has consistently shown negative emotions hinder success for individuals looking to improve.
In practice, weekly check-ins are one of the most important places to use positive affirmations. Specifically, in the beginning of the fitness professional–client relationship, it is important to foster an unconditionally positive and supportive environment. These early stages help clients hear and realize the things they are doing right, building their confidence as well as strengthening the bond with their fitness professional. Fitness professionals need to promote an open and honest relationship in which the client is unafraid to share their struggles and stumbles because they know they will not be met with judgment or shame but, instead, guidance and understanding. Through the fitness professional’s continued positive affirmation, clients will often start to develop their own positive self-affirmations and observations, which will shape their future behaviors and decisions.

### Empathetic Approach

Chapter 4Communication and Coaching StrategiesLesson 2Virtual Accountability StrategiesEmpathetic ApproachEmpathy is the ability to imagine oneself in the position of another and to relate to a person from this frame of reference while trying to understand why they think and feel the way they do when viewed from their standpoint and experiences.
The ability of a fitness professional to empathize with their client can have a profound effect on the client—fitness professional relationship. A client’s perception of a fitness professional’s empathy may be a better predictor of positive client outcomes than how knowledgeable the fitness professional is in their respective field (Watson et al., 2014). Empathy should be used selectively and with careful reserve.
arloo/Shutterstock.comProfessionals should not presume to know exactly what a client feels or has experienced, as it is impossible to truly experience life through anyone else’s eyes. Through the cultivation of great interpersonal communication skills, reflection, and visualization, a fitness professional can try to piece together the life and experiences of their client.
Fitness professionals often attempt to draw on their own experiences, knowledge, and education to try and find solutions to the unique problems each client faces. Taking the time and care to try to understand a client from a more holistic point-of-view can help fitness professionals guide clients through their journey to improved health more effectively. It is true the most theoretically sound fitness and nutrition plan will be useless if it cannot be applied and executed by the individual for whom it was programmed. In this instance, the client is the confounding variable. The capacity for empathy coupled with the discerning ability to apply it and when can determine the spectrum of individuals with whom fitness professionals can achieve results.

### Virtual Motivational Interviewing

Chapter 4Communication and Coaching Strategies Lesson 3Virtual Motivational InterviewingVirtual Motivational Interviewing Motivational interviewing is an empathetic, collaborative, and client-centered approach to communication. It is designed to help direct clients through ambivalence and support positive change. In these instances, ambivalence may be expressed in intermittent indifference to the client’s own goals or changing of focus. Motivational interviewing helps shift the focus by asking questions in such a way as to evoke thoughtfulness and reflection from clients to help them find their own reasons for change and motivation, in a non-judgmental, compassionate manner.
Motivational interviewing has been found to be especially effective in helping clients achieve change through health and fitness (Armstrong et al., 2011) Virtual motivational interviewing is similar to the in-person experience. It may take extra time to do so, but those details can create a vastly different experience for both the client and the fitness professional and, often, can lead to a more meaningful, successful, and longer-term working relationship.

### Behavioral Change

Chapter 4Communication and Coaching Strategies Lesson 3Virtual Motivational InterviewingBehavioral ChangeChanging behaviors is difficult. Many barriers to change exist in both physical and mental paths. All individuals have their own composition of fear, education, environment, social settings, and time constraints that create different outcomes and building blocks for the same problems. To help clients find what approach will work for them, fitness professionals first need to help them find the root of these issues so the proper maps can be created to alter the course and path of their future. As presented by the Stages of Change Model or Transtheoretical Model of Change (Figure: Stages of Change), developed in the 1970’s by Prochaska and DiClemente, there are five stages of change:
FigureStages of Change
    Precontemplation – At this stage, an individual is not yet aware or is unwilling to accept that a change is needed. They are likely hanging on to their current behaviors and have not yet found intrinsic motivation to change but may have pressure from other sources to make change.
    Contemplation – The client has started to become aware that a change is needed, but they are struggling with the pros and cons of doing so, the amount of effort that may be required of them, or what they may have to sacrifice to see change.
    Preparation – This is the stage where serious consideration starts to take place and even a desire to begin taking steps to make change. At this point, they are starting to think about what they can do to bring about change but may still be struggling with knowing where to begin. The desire for change is there.
    Action – Here they start taking physical action, moving beyond the stages of mental effort to actually taking steps to implement the changes that, until this point, have only been considerations. For many, this will be the most difficult stage as it is the “first step,” so to speak, and where they will have to put forth the greatest commitment to keep momentum.
    Maintenance – At this point, the client has been consistent with their new behaviors for at least 6 months (Rubak et al., 2005) and it is becoming an integrated part of their lifestyle. Getting here does not mean they will stay here, though, and, if they have not been focused on a sustainable approach, they are still at risk of relapse to old behaviors.

In reality, individuals will often shift back and forth between various stages of change as they encounter new obstacles or things in their lives change, and, although they can progress through the stages in a linear fashion, that may not be typical.

### Client Ambivalence

Chapter 4Communication and Coaching Strategies Lesson 3Virtual Motivational InterviewingClient AmbivalenceIt is also common throughout the coaching process to encounter client ambivalence, where the client has reservations about certain changes, which will vary in reason from person to person. Ambivalence is a state of mind where an individual may have conflict, disinterest, or resistance regarding an outcome or individual.
For example, ambivalence can present, at times, revolving around nutrition where the client is overwhelmed at the notion of learning to eat by hunger cues or perhaps eating at set mealtimes. Altering habits are not easy and resistance is part of change. In another example, if a client wants to participate in a specific training program but cannot due to time constraints, their enthusiasm may decrease while ambivalence increases because their “perfect” schedule cannot be achieved. If they have the perception they are unable to get results on one program, they may choose to do no program at all.
Often, a major point of friction and cause for ambivalence from clients comes from the desire to see results while maintaining their current lifestyles. Individuals want fast results, change, and instant fixes rather than the uncomfortable process of self-evaluation and exploration. It can be difficult for them to accept their current state due to past and current choices, beliefs, and behaviors. Acceptance can be overwhelming at times, but it is imperative to achieve long-term change. By altering and redirecting focus, the client may get to a place they might not have reached previously.

### Motivational Interviewing in Practice

Chapter 4Communication and Coaching Strategies Lesson 3Virtual Motivational InterviewingMotivational Interviewing in Practice With motivational interviewing, fitness professionals want to create an environment where clients feel unconditionally supported and safe to express their feelings. If they feel the fitness professional lacks empathy or is judgmental, they may become closed off and less motivated to share or continue. These can result in what is known as discord. Discord is a breach in the fitness professional–client relationship, which can pull attention away from the original relationship intention.
Discord can decrease willingness to change, and, if a client is already prone to sustain talk, it can increase the perception that change is simply too hard. This is the opposite of what professionals want. When clients express sustain talk, they need to feel heard. The fitness professional should use open-ended questions and empathizing to redirect the conversation back toward change talk. ### Summary

Chapter 4Communication and Coaching Strategies Lesson 3Virtual Motivational InterviewingAs much as possible, fitness professionals want the relationship between them and their clients to be a collaborative one. Professionals should help clients explore the deeper reasoning behind their obstacles, find a lifestyle that supports their values and goals in balance, and develop the tools to self-evaluate, self-monitor, reflect, and adapt. This is precisely why motivational interviewing, empathy, and great communication are crucial skills to develop as fitness professionals. If the fitness professional and client work together to develop bespoke approaches to goal achievement, it is likely that the client can sustain for the long-term. It is up to fitness professionals to help clients navigate and invest in their current and future health, rather than find a temporary goal and regress back to their old habits. The goal must be realistically attainable as well as sustainable.
Whatever the reasons for using motivational interviewing, it can help clients move past ambivalence to change talk by allowing them to explore their reasoning in an environment that is safe from judgement and encourages them to align their behaviors to their values.
Summary Virtual communication is a relatively new platform and environment for fitness professionals and clients all over the world to come together and interact. More recently and more frequently, there are an abundance of opportunities for fitness professionals to work with and help clients from a distance, regardless of physical proximity. Fitness professionals have a variety of options for working with clients virtually, from email to web and mobile-based software applications and asynchronous or synchronous communication strategies—enabling them to find the right fit for clients and themselves. Fitness professionals help their clients through the process of planning and setting goals and then achieving those goals through an individualized, tailored, and achievable process. Through an organized process of data collection and analysis, and consistency in communication, fitness professionals can use their knowledge, skills, abilities, and talents to keep clients accountable and work through periods of issues of ambivalence and hardship. Ultimately, fitness professionals will use empathy and evidence-based methods to help clients realize and achieve their own goals and dreams, all while building an unshakable relationship, which can influence and enhance the lives of the client and fitness professional for a lifetime.
