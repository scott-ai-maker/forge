### Differences in Wearable Devices

Chapter 7Integrating Wearables Into Virtual CoachingLesson 1Introduction to Fitness WearablesDifferences in Wearable DevicesPeter Kotoff/Shutterstock.comWearable technologies have many differences, including the type of embedded sensors, method of data collection, what they measure, advanced features, and the quality and reliability of the data. When integrating wearable devices into a virtual coaching business, fitness professionals should be able to adapt and work with a device already owned by a client, but also be prepared to make suggestions based on the most suitable option for their needs.
Getting TechnicalElectrocardiography (ECG) Versus Photoplethysmography (PPG) in Wearable Technology 

Electrocardiography (ECG) is a measurement of electrical activity at the heart that controls the expansion and contraction of the heart chambers, often using multiple electrodes. ECG is the gold standard measurement used in hospitals and healthcare settings to monitor heart rate and heart rate variability.

Photoplethysmography (PPG) uses light-based technology to measure the rate of blood flow as controlled by the heart’s pumping action. In addition to measurements of heart rate, analysis of the PPG waveform can provide an accurate estimation of additional biometrics, depending on the wavelength or color of light utilized.

### Green Versus Red Light PPG

Chapter 7Integrating Wearables Into Virtual CoachingLesson 1Introduction to Fitness WearablesGreen Versus Red Light PPG Most wearable devices utilize green light PPG. Measurements done on light-skinned individuals at normal temperature have shown that green light has desired properties for data collection, particularly during motion (Maeda et al., 2011). Green light PPG has been shown to be more readily absorbed by individuals with higher melanin levels (Shcherbina et al., 2017), including those with darker skin tones, which can often lead to inaccuracies in data collection.
Red and infrared (IR) light PPG sensors, while less reliable during physical activity, have various benefits related to deeper penetration in the skin (Figure: Green/Red Light PPG) and typically provide more reliable data regardless of skin tone (Spigulis, Gailite, Lihachev, & Erts, 2007). To correct for inaccuracies caused by motion in red light PPG sensors, many wearable technology companies have created algorithms that utilize the accelerometer to produce high-quality PPG signals. For these adaptive filters, the accelerometer movement data is used to detect and subtract motion from the PPG signal or to exclude the data completely if an accuracy threshold cannot be obtained.
FigureGreen/Red Light PPGAdvanced FeaturesThe features and biometric data collected from wearable devices will vary by manufacturer and even by device offered by the same wearable manufacturer. The number of advanced features often increases with the cost of the device, including color screens, onboard GPS, and waterproofing. More expensive is not always better, as many features may be unnecessary or lead to additional confusion for the client. Fitness professionals must understand what each device offers. They must know how the device relates to their ability to develop and execute effective virtual coaching and consider the balance between cost, features, and ease of use for the client.

### Data Reliability

Chapter 7Integrating Wearables Into Virtual CoachingLesson 1Introduction to Fitness WearablesData ReliabilityAndrey_Popov/Shutterstock.comWhen relied on for risk stratification, program development, and client monitoring, the accuracy of data collected is of the utmost importance. In a comparative study of six mainstream wearable fitness trackers, researchers found that “Fitness trackers of different brands vary with regard to the measurement of indicators and are all affected by the activity state” (Xie et al., 2018).
All wearable technologies will have some margin of error relative to costly and bulky medical equipment. However, using the same device consistently will allow the client to establish a baseline activity level and show the fitness professional a developing trend to support improvements and adjustments. In general, fitness professionals should select a reliable device, avoid switching between different devices, and focus on establishing and monitoring data trends over time.
Coach’s CornerSelecting a Reliable Wearable Technology

When determining which wearable technology to integrate into a virtual coaching business, fitness professionals should research which devices have been clinically validated versus gold standard medical equipment. For example, heart rate and/or oxygen saturation should be validated against a hospital-grade electrocardiogram or pulse oximeter and not against other commercial devices lacking validation.

Most commercial wearable technologies do not have the approval of the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA approval) but may have a research section available on their company website. Additionally, fitness professionals may search for scholarly articles on Google Scholar, JSTOR, or a comparable resource for information about data reliability of certain devices.

Heart rate is most commonly assessed at rest and during physical activity, both of which should be considered independently. Critical!The Role of a Fitness Professional

A fitness professional is not credentialed to diagnose, treat, or prevent any specific medical conditions. While many of these biometrics may provide insight into potential health issues, the role of wearable technology in virtual coaching is to provide reliable data for program development. Fitness professionals should not provide medical advice but, rather, recommend that clients seek medical attention from a qualified health professional if alarming biometric values or trends are recognized, especially if accompanied by symptoms including chest pain, shortness of breath, dizziness, or fatigue.

### Resting Heart Rate

Chapter 7Integrating Wearables Into Virtual Coaching Lesson 2Biometric EducationResting Heart RateResting heart rate (RHR) is the number of beats per minute obtained during rest. A normal resting heart rate can vary widely from person to person, but the accepted range for adults is 60 to 100 bpm (American Heart Association, 2015) (Figure: Resting Heart Rate).
FigureResting Heart RateHowever, individuals with higher resting heart rate values within this range may still be at increased risk of cardiovascular, non-cardiovascular, and all-cause mortality (Seccareccia et al., 2001). An elevated resting heart rate above 100 bpm, referred to as tachycardia, can be indicative of an underlying health condition that should be addressed by a trained healthcare professional.
In general, a lower resting heart rate is related to improved heart function and cardiovascular fitness (Nauman et al., 2012). A well-trained individual may maintain a resting heart rate well below 60 bpm, referred to as bradycardia, due to positive cardiovascular adaptations from training. For clients who are not physically active but still maintain a heart rate below 60 bpm, fitness professionals should prompt the client to seek approval from a doctor prior to engaging in physical activity, as this can be a sign of an underlying heart condition (American Heart Association, 2016).
When assessed properly, resting heart rate should be relatively stable for all populations with minimal day-to-day changes. Over time, positive or negative lifestyle interventions may lead to a steady change in resting heart rate values. In a meta-analysis, Reimers et al. (2018) found that all types of exercise, especially endurance training and yoga, decreased resting heart rate in healthy subjects, which may contribute to a reduction in all-cause mortality.

### Heart Rate Variability

Chapter 7Integrating Wearables Into Virtual Coaching Lesson 2Biometric EducationHeart Rate Variability Heart rate variability (HRV) is the measurement of variation in time intervals between heartbeats, most commonly reported in milliseconds (ms). Wearable devices are frequently limited to time-domain measurements of HRV and report values as the standard deviation of N-N intervals (SDNN) or root mean squared standard deviation (RMSSD). Additional measures of HRV include nonlinear or frequency-domain analysis, which is beyond the scope of this discussion.

HRV provides quantifiable and non-invasive insight into the current state of a client’s autonomic nervous system, which is involved in the regulation of autonomic or involuntary processes. The autonomic nervous system consists of two primary branches, sympathetic (fight-or-flight) and parasympathetic (rest, digest, and relax). HRV is a measurement of the ongoing contention between these two branches to regulate physiological processes without conscious thought. For example, an individual does not have to actively think about breathing, their heart beating, or maintaining blood pressure due to autonomic control of these processes. More importantly, though, recent research has shown that changes in HRV may be related to inflammatory processes, hormone regulation, and immune system function (Liu et al., 2017).

Chapter 7Integrating Wearables Into Virtual Coaching Lesson 2Biometric EducationCritical!Heart Rate Variability and Health

Due to its relation to physical and mental health outcomes, HRV is among the most meaningful biometrics that fitness professionals should consider when developing and measuring the success of a lifestyle intervention program. There is growing evidence that various diseases are accompanied by reduced HRV values including, but not limited to, the following (Young & Benton, 2018):


    Diabetes
    Cardiovascular disease
    Psychiatric disorders
    All-cause mortality


In a meta-analysis, researchers found that individuals with lower HRV values compared to their cohort had a 112% higher risk of all-cause death and a 46% increased incidence of cardiovascular events (Fang et al., 2019).
Heart rate variability is a highly individualized biometric and should not be utilized to compare different clients but, instead, assessed for positive or negative change at the individual level. While HRV has been shown to decrease predictably with age (Jandackova et al., 2016) and various health-related conditions, the value of monitoring HRV in a virtual coaching program is to determine an individual’s recovery, daily training readiness, and monitor progress over time. In general, HRV values should be relatively stable from day to day but may increase gradually over time with positive lifestyle interventions, including improved sleep, exercise activity, and nutritional habits. Higher HRV values have been positively associated with improved cardiovascular fitness and stress resilience (Dong, 2016) and decreased risk of training-related injuries (Williams et al., 2017). Additional studies have shown that decreased heart rate variability is an effective tool for measuring cumulative physical fatigue in overtrained athletes (Pichot et al., 2000), which may offer insight into programming periods of lower intensity to prioritize recovery (Figure: Heart Rate Variability).
FigureHeart Rate VariabilityCoach’s CornerGuiding Training With HRV 

HRV represents the current state of the individual’s autonomic nervous system and its ability to make a positive adaptation. By assessing an individual’s HRV trend and adjusting daily training efforts accordingly, fitness professionals can help clients train smarter while also improving overall outcomes.

Training Prescription Guided by HRV in Cycling (Javaloyes et al. 2018)

In a study of elite cyclists, researchers divided participants into two groups: an HRV-guided training group and a traditional periodization group. The HRV-guided training group had their daily output dynamically adjusted based on daily HRV values, whereas the traditional periodization group followed a structured protocol. Following an 8-week training intervention, researchers found that HRV-guided training led to greater increases in the following:


    Peak power (5%)
    Power at second ventilatory threshold (VT2) (14%)
    Power over a 40-minute time trial (7%)

### Oxygen Saturation

Chapter 7Integrating Wearables Into Virtual Coaching Lesson 2Biometric EducationOxygen Saturation When an individual breathes, oxygen is attached to and distributed throughout the body by a protein molecule called hemoglobin in the red blood cells. Oxygen saturation, often referred to as SpO2 and reported as a percentage (%) value, is the fraction of oxygenated hemoglobin relative to the total amount of hemoglobin. The normal range for oxygen saturation in healthy adults is 95 to 100 percent at rest (Figure: Normal Oxygen Saturation) but can be as low as 88 to 92 percent in clients with known conditions such as Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease or other lung diseases (Holland, 2019). Lifestyle habits such as smoking may also reduce oxygen saturation (Ozdal et al., 2017).
FigureNormal Oxygen SaturationMonitoring oxygen saturation is important because low blood oxygen (hypoxemia) can lead to adverse effects on important organ systems, including the brain, heart, and kidneys (Hafen & Sharma, 2020). In general, clients with normal oxygen saturation should not recognize significant day-to-day changes or overtime with program interventions. If a significant decrease in oxygen saturation is recognized, particularly if it is accompanied by symptoms, fitness professionals should urge clients to seek medical attention.
HemoglobinA red protein responsible for transporting oxygen in the blood of vertebrates.

### Respiratory Rate

Chapter 7Integrating Wearables Into Virtual Coaching Lesson 2Biometric EducationRespiratory RateRespiratory rate, measured in breaths per minute (bpm), is the number of breaths an individual takes in 1 minute at rest. Respiration is measured as one complete inhale and exhale, which represents the cycle of respiratory gas exchange of oxygen and carbon dioxide. Normal ranges for respiratory rates vary throughout the life cycle (Figure: Respiratory Rates by Age), with newborns breathing at a rate of up to 60 breaths per minute but leveling out at a steady rate of 12 to 20 breaths per minute in adulthood (Nall, 2017). For adults, respiratory rates below 12 or over 25 are considered abnormal and may be indicative of an underlying condition such as asthma, anxiety, pneumonia, heart, or lung disease (Cleveland Clinic, 2019). Respiratory rate may also be influenced by posture or mechanical restrictions of the diaphragm (Zafar et al., 2018).
FigureRespiratory Rates by AgeBased on physical activity and other stressors, respiratory rate can vary significantly throughout the day. Fitness professionals should focus on nocturnal measurements of the respiratory rate due to the relatively controlled environment. In general, the nocturnal respiratory rate should be steady from day to day with minimal change over time. Minor changes in nocturnal respiratory rate may be indicative of recent alcohol consumption (Izumi et al., 2005), whereas rapid and significant changes may be indicative of illness requiring medical attention (Cretikos et al., 2008).
Coach’s CornerAssessing Respiratory Rate During Activity 

In response to physical activity, the autonomic nervous system will increase the respiratory rate to account for increased oxygen demand and a change in blood pH due to the increased concentration of carbon dioxide. Changes in respiratory rate measured during physical activity have been shown to be a valid and reliable marker for assessing cardiorespiratory fitness and anaerobic threshold during a graded exercise test (Carey et al., 2005), which may be valuable for program development. Unfortunately, most wearable devices are intolerant to motion and do not provide a reliable measurement of respiratory rate during physical activity.
Respiratory rateThe number of breaths an individual takes in 1 minute at rest, typically measured in breaths per minute (bpm).

Critical!Circadian Rhythm 

One of the most meaningful, simple, and cost-effective (free) interventions that a fitness professional can recommend is adherence to a consistent sleep cycle. The human body has evolved to follow a natural and internal 24-hour clock, or circadian rhythm, which impacts body temperature, metabolism, and hormonal processes (National Institute of General Medical Sciences, 2020). Physiological processes are optimized when factors such as sleep and wake up times are consistently maintained. Consistency in sleep patterns has been correlated with improved cognitive function and memory, whereas inconsistent circadian patterns have shown to increase the incidence of illness, cardiovascular disease, and cancer (Scheiermann et al., 2013). The human body’s natural circadian rhythm can be disrupted by external factors, including temperature and light.

Fitness professionals may consider circadian rhythm interventions or recommendations to maintain consistent sleep onset and wake up times to improve an individual’s overall sleep quality and performance.

Regarding the common biometrics provided by wearable devices, fitness professionals should assess recovery and readiness based on the biometrics that have been clinically validated to predict the risk of injury, daily readiness to perform, or change in health status. ### Process Overview

FigureProgram DesignIntegrating Wearables Into Virtual CoachingPhase
Overview/Description
1 – Baseline
Establish a minimum of a 3- to 5-day baseline to obtain objective insight into a client’s biometric strengths and deficiencies. During the baseline phase, the client should be instructed to continue with normal lifestyle behaviors and not adjust nutritional or exercise habits.
2 – Analysis and Initial Program Development
Following the baseline phase, fitness professionals should perform a comprehensive analysis of a client’s strengths and deficiencies in relation to their goals. Programs should be developed to address deficiencies that may limit program success while ensuring that strengths are not sacrificed. A comprehensive program is developed and provided to the client with an explanation of WHY specific interventions were chosen.
3 – Ongoing Monitoring
Fitness professionals should develop a consistent schedule for monitoring client data. While it may not be feasible to assess data every day, it is important to check in on days that rigorous training is scheduled to assess readiness and, if necessary, adjust program recommendations. Some wearable platforms provide automated alerts of significant change to streamline the monitoring process.
4 – Adjust & Consult
Utilize biometric data to dynamically adjust program recommendations based on client recovery and readiness to safely and effectively perform the recommended program for the day. Over time, fitness professionals may be able to create a more effective program schedule based on recovery trends from previous exercise interventions.

If a program adjustment is required, fitness professionals should attempt to understand the unexpected change in biometric data. For example, poor sleep quality and decreased HRV may be explained if the client consumed alcohol the evening prior. This represents an opportunity to show the client how this behavior impacted their biometrics to create awareness and avoid future deviations from the program.

In the case that positive biometric trends are recognized, this represents an opportunity to provide positive reinforcement to maintain program adherence.

Coach’s CornerProgram Development – Practical Example

Client A is a 45-year-old male with a desk job and a high-level of work-related stress. After a check-up from his doctor, Client A was diagnosed as pre-diabetic and obese. Client A’s physician cleared him for exercise but emphasized the importance of lifestyle changes. Client A has tried to exercise and improve his nutritional habits but gives up because the scale weight does not improve.

Following a comprehensive assessment and establishing a 5-day biometric baseline, the fitness professional identified the following deficiencies:


    2400 steps/day
    Sleep latency over 30 minutes
    Lack of consistency in sleep times
    Elevated resting heart rate (88 bpm)
    Reduced HRV (28 ms)


Based on the fitness professional’s qualifications, it is determined that a minimally invasive and phase-based program will be the most effective and sustainable for Client A:


    Daily step count goal of 5000 steps, increased by 500 steps per week: To emphasize consistency of movement throughout the day, Client A is instructed to set an alarm and take at least 500 steps every hour during the workday.
    Exercise program (based on other assessments)
    Nutritional counseling (based on nutritional assessment forms)
    Sleep recommendations and Circadian Rhythm Intervention: Avoid use of technology for 1 hour prior to bed, create a bedtime routine, and set consistent sleep and wake times each day.
    Education: Why these biometrics are important, expected improvements, and the significance to Client A’s health and goals


The fitness professional will monitor Client A’s biometric data for compliance and consistently provide positive reinforcement based on improvements in sleep quality, daily physical activity, and other applicable biometrics.

### Data-Driven Adjustments

Chapter 7Integrating Wearables Into Virtual CoachingLesson 3Building a ProgramData-Driven AdjustmentsHuman physiology is complex and adaptable. This requires fitness professionals to consistently monitor and dynamically adjust programs to minimize the risk of training-related accidents, avoid injuries, and optimize client outcomes. The use of wearable technology provides fitness professionals with the unprecedented ability to objectively quantify and track the efficacy of any type or combination of interventions. For example, a fitness professional with the proper qualifications may develop a similar nutrition program for two individuals who present with similar backgrounds and biometrics but recognize the high probability of significantly different health and body composition outcomes.

    Adjusting the type or intensity of a single exercise session based on recovery data
    Adjusting a portion of an intervention to test if outcomes can be improved, such as modifying macronutrient recommendations
    Removing an intervention that does not seem to be yielding outcomes
    Removing an intervention that is negatively affecting biometric data
    Adding an intervention based on changes in biometric data over time
    Dynamic modification of a program to avoid plateaus


For example, a previously sedentary client following an exercise intervention may require additional sleep to facilitate adequate recovery.

### Behavioral Considerations

Chapter 7Integrating Wearables Into Virtual CoachingLesson 3Building a ProgramBehavioral ConsiderationsThe basic principles of human behavior state that behaviors that are reinforced tend to be maintained. Clients have likely attempted lifestyle modification on their own prior to seeking help from a fitness professional but failed because they did not recognize improvements and could not maintain intrinsic motivation. Utilizing biometric data from wearable devices provides fitness professionals with the ability to consistently monitor compliance, which is a notable limitation of virtual coaching, and provides a source of positive reinforcement to optimize ongoing momentum for improvement (Figure: Reinforcement Theory of Motivation). Fitness professionals should actively pursue any opportunity to make a client feel good about their progress, whether it be based on compliance to a program or positive biometric trends related to overall health and performance (Expert Program Management, 2018).
FigureReinforcement Theory of MotivationAdditionally, biometric data can be utilized to bring attention to the negative impact of certain behaviors. For example, a decrease in sleep quality and heart rate variability following a night of heavy alcohol consumption may decrease the likelihood of the individual deviating from the program in the future.

Lastly, clients want to know that they are being supported and monitored by a fitness professional they can trust. By integrating wearable technology into virtual coaching, fitness professionals have the ability to further individualize recommendations and continuity of care with 24/7 monitoring beyond what can be offered in a training facility.
Positive reinforcementA stimulus following a behavior that increases the likelihood of an individual continuing that behavior – For example, improvements in scale weight due to nutritional intervention increase the likelihood of an individual continuing to adhere to the nutrition program.
