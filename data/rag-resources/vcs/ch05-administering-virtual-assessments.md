### Introduction

Chapter 5Administering Virtual AssessmentsLesson 1Virtual Pre-Participation Health ScreeningIntroductionThe role of a fitness professional is to support clients in achieving their health and fitness goals in the safest, most efficient manner possible. Assessments—and subsequently, reassessments—are integral components to this process. Fitness professionals will administer a variety of assessments with clients to establish a starting point for their program and a baseline from which to measure and track progress toward goals over time. With some modifications and additional resources, these assessments can be conducted virtually without compromising safety, utility, and effectiveness.
Le Panda/Shutterstock.comWhile clients are typically able to define their own health and fitness goals, they often lack an objective understanding of their baseline starting point. Considering this, in order to develop an effective, individualized program, fitness professionals must utilize both objective and subjective assessment tools to gain a better understanding of a client’s baseline strengths and areas of opportunity with respect to their goal/s, medical history, exercise history, current levels of fitness and conditioning, recreational or sport activities, and/or previous injuries. Following a phase of analysis, this assessment and reassessment data can be utilized to develop and modify individualized programs, monitor progress toward goals and objectives, and be leveraged to provide reinforcement for adherence and retention.
Administering assessments is common practice for the trained fitness professional. When performed remotely, there are considerations and modifications necessary to maintain safety and reliability, which will be discussed in this chapter. Fitness professionals should rely on additional resources and technologies, when applicable, for administering virtual assessments.
Helpful Hint Navigating a Client’s Health and Fitness Journey:

To develop the most efficient route (program), a client must define the intended destination (goal) but also have a precise understanding of the starting location (baseline). As the client is en route to their destination, navigation may recommend a slight detour (program modification made based on reassessment) with the ultimate goal of reaching the destination in the safest and most efficient manner possible.
ObjectiveAn assessment based on facts or direct measurement, such as a quantitative measurement.
SubjectiveAn assessment based on individual opinion or perception, which lacks the ability to directly measure.
ReliabilityThe accuracy of a measurement or the degree to which the measurement can be relied upon.

### Administering Pre-Participation Health Screening

Chapter 5Administering Virtual AssessmentsLesson 1Virtual Pre-Participation Health ScreeningAdministering Pre-Participation Health ScreeningImportant components of program development include safety and sustainability. There are various types of health screening tools available, which can be efficiently conducted via email, phone call, or video conferencing. ### Physical Activity Readiness Questionnaire for Everyone (PAR-Q+)

Chapter 5Administering Virtual AssessmentsLesson 1Virtual Pre-Participation Health ScreeningPhysical Activity Readiness Questionnaire for Everyone (PAR-Q+)The Physical Activity Readiness Questionnaire for Everyone (PAR-Q+) is a standardized assessment of a client’s readiness to engage in physical activity. Fitness professionals should avoid making assumptions or relying on aesthetic characteristics alone when determining physical readiness for any type of program or intervention. Due to the personal and sensitive nature of the questions, the PAR-Q+ is generally self-administered with minimal guidance. Instructions are included in the PAR-Q+, but a fitness professional should consider providing a brief overview and instructions.

The PAR-Q+ consists entirely of yes or no answers, with instructions to complete additional sections based on responses. In order to be considered valid, the PAR-Q+ does require a participant signature. For this reason, clients may consider printing and scanning the completed document, which may be done with a scanner or personal camera and sent via email or text message (Figure: Technology for PAR-Q+).
FigureTechnology for PAR-Q+Food for ThoughtPAR-Q Versus PAR-Q+

Among the most accepted and widely used health screening surveys is the PAR-Q+. The original PAR-Q, created by the British Columbia Ministry of Health and the Multidisciplinary Board on Exercise, was developed as a standardized assessment to gauge an individual’s readiness to perform physical activity. Based on feedback from individuals engaging in physical activity, fitness professionals, and medical physicians, researchers found that the original PAR-Q had limitations in terms of utility and effectiveness across the broad population, citing that the established health benefits of participation far outweigh the risks (Bredin, Gledhill, Jamnik & Warburton, 2013).

Whereas the PAR-Q unnecessarily restricted exercise for populations who would benefit from physical activity, the revised PAR-Q+ was developed to be more inclusive, with the + indicating “For Everyone.”

### Administering Physiological Assessments Virtually

Chapter 5Administering Virtual AssessmentsLesson 2Virtual Physiological AssessmentsAdministering Physiological Assessments VirtuallyIn addition to survey information, an effective program should be developed based on individualized physiological data. Physiological measurements are those that objectively quantify certain characteristics, including body weight, circumference measurements, and vital signs. Fitness professionals should focus on education and committable habits to help clients understand the importance of accurately measuring physiological data. Leveraging cost-effective and readily available technology will improve data reliability and avoid human error in data collection and/or reporting.
Physiological AssessmentsIt is important to understand that physiological assessments are sensitive to the external environment, influenced by physical exertion, and may fluctuate significantly throughout the day. Fitness professionals should consciously avoid confounds during physiological assessments. As a general rule, physiological assessments should be performed in the following ways:

    First thing in the morning, ideally at the same time each day, in the same outfit
    In the same position each time – Results can be significantly different in a seated versus standing position.
    No earlier than 2 hours following exercise or a stressful event
    No earlier than 1 hour after consuming caffeine
    Using the same data collection method, instructions, and equipment


Fitness professionals should leverage technology and resources to expedite and improve the reliability of virtual physiological assessments. In addition to comprehensive—yet simple and easy-to-understand—written instructions provided via e-mail or text message, fitness professionals may consider pre-recording instructional videos or dedicating time to walk clients through these assessments with live video conferencing, coaching, and demonstration (Figure: Technology for Physiological Assessments).

### Resting Heart Rate

Chapter 5Administering Virtual AssessmentsLesson 2Virtual Physiological Assessments Resting Heart RateResting heart rate, measured in beats per minute, is a common physiological assessment to non-invasively gauge an individual’s health and fitness. According to a study of nearly 3,000 male subjects followed over 16 years, researchers concluded that elevated resting heart rate is a risk factor for mortality independent of physical fitness and other major cardiovascular risk factors (Jensen et al. 2013). For this reason, fitness professionals should consistently assess resting heart rate to monitor progress or changes in health status.

Fitness professionals should take the time to guide their clients through a baseline resting heart rate assessment through visual demonstration when possible, which can be accomplished through pre-recorded instructional videos sent by e-mail or a live video conference (Figure: Technology for Resting Heart Rate).

### Heart Rate Recovery

Chapter 5Administering Virtual AssessmentsLesson 2Virtual Physiological AssessmentsHeart Rate RecoveryHeart Rate Recovery (HRR) is a measurement of a client’s heart rate after a defined period of time following intense exercise. For example, if a client were to perform an exercise until they achieve an active heart rate of 160 beats per minute, and then, after 1 minute of no exercise, their heart rate returns to 145 beats per minute, this client would have a heart rate recovery of 15 beats per minute. In general, a higher value for heart rate recovery is preferred, as this is correlated with improved fitness and overall cardiovascular health (Dimkpa, 2009).
The method for assessing heart rate recovery should be determined based on the client’s baseline physical fitness and activity level and should always be performed under the guidance of a trained fitness professional. For example, a less-active or older client may be guided through an incremental walk test, where the intensity or speed is gradually increased and the rate of perceived exertion is consistently assessed by the fitness professional. To ensure safety, it is recommended that the fitness professional perform this assessment via video conference or phone call to guide the user through the protocol and assist with recording the heart rate measurements (Figure: Technology for Heart Rate Recovery).

If the client is a well-trained athlete, a fitness professional may consider a more rigorous protocol, such as a functional threshold power assessment. In this population, the client may be more likely to have a heart rate monitor and can accurately and safely perform this assessment without much guidance, although it is still recommended that this be conducted via video conference.

In both cases, the method for assessing heart rate recovery should be standardized. Following exercise, the client may stand up or sit down during the recovery period, but the fitness professional should document this and ensure that future reassessments are performed under the same conditions as this may have an effect on the results.

### Blood Pressure

Chapter 5Administering Virtual AssessmentsLesson 2Virtual Physiological AssessmentsBlood PressureBased on the results of the PAR-Q+ and Health History Questionnaire, it may be necessary to prompt clients to regularly assess their blood pressure. There are many affordable over-the-counter blood pressure devices available, including cuff-based devices worn at the wrist or upper arm. While wrist devices have advantages in terms of size, cost, and fitting, it is important to consider data integrity of such an important physiological assessment. In a population-based study, researchers found that “use of wrist devices for home self-measurement leads to frequent detection of falsely elevated blood pressure values” (Casiglia et al.2016). For this reason, fitness professionals are encouraged to recommend traditional upper-arm monitors.

Prior to assessing blood pressure, clients should be instructed to sit at rest for a minimum of 5 minutes. The following steps are important to reliably measure blood pressure, regardless of the device manufacturer:
Andrey_Popov/Shutterstock.comFIGURETechnology for Blood Pressure
    Remove clothing or anything preventing direct contact between the cuff and skin.
    Apply the cuff, making sure that the indicator line is placed directly over the artery.
    Ensure that the elbow is placed comfortably on a stationary object (such as a desk) with the forearm horizontal across the body. The wrist bone should be at heart level.
    Avoid excessive movement during the blood pressure measurement.


Fitness professionals should take the time to guide their clients through baseline blood pressure monitoring via video conferencing or pre-recorded instructional videos to ensure instructions are closely followed to optimize data reliability (Figure: Technology for Blood Pressure). If abnormal values are reported, fitness professionals should consider the live video conference method with step-by-step instructions and feedback to ensure reliable values are obtained.

### Anthropometric Assessments

Chapter 5Administering Virtual AssessmentsLesson 2Virtual Physiological AssessmentsAnthropometric AssessmentsAnthropometric assessments are quantitative measurements of the physical body with commonly assessed components, including height, weight, body mass index (BMI), and body circumference (Casadei & Kiel, 2020). These measurements are generally simple to obtain and can be used as a tool to monitor progress, provide reinforcement and motivation, and dynamically adjust programs.
Scale WeightScale weight is among the most common assessment tools available. While it may be simple to instruct a client to step on a scale and write down a number, there are many considerations that impact the reliability of scale weight measurements. The most important consideration for scale weight is consistency.

Due to daily fluctuations in weight throughout the day based on drinking fluids, consuming food, and using the restroom, that simple number on the scale can quickly turn into a less-meaningful tool to assess progress and may lead to discouragement. Fitness professionals may consider averaging client weight over 2 to 4 weeks to account for these normal fluctuations and for a more reliable measurement of change in scale weight over time, particularly in women who may experience weight gain during the menstrual cycle. To facilitate the communication and monitoring of scale weight results, fitness professionals may create a Google Sheet or use another spreadsheet program for immediate manual entry following measurement. Additionally, fitness professionals can instruct clients to immediately report results via e-mail or text message (Figure: Technology for Scale Weight).
9dream studio/Shutterstock.comFIGURETechnology for Scale WeightIn general, a fitness professional should instruct their client to weigh themselves first thing in the morning, prior to the first meal, wearing minimal clothing or the same outfit. Fitness professionals should consider placing less emphasis on scale weight and shifting validation of program success to other assessments that accurately reflect progress, such as photos and circumference measurements.
Critical! Placing too much emphasis on scale weight can be counterproductive. According to Dionne & Yeudall (2005),

Attention focused on one’s current, and presumably undesirable, body size can be motivating to those who want to modify a pattern of unhealthy behavior. For others, this feedback may result in psychological distress and could lead to outcomes that are, in fact, counter to a weight loss strategy, such as attrition from programs, misreporting of dietary intake, and emotional states associated with binge eating.

A fitness professional should consider the client type and program goal to determine how often measurements such as scale weight should be recorded as well as the value to the functional goal of the client in general.

### Body Mass Index

Chapter 5Administering Virtual AssessmentsLesson 2Virtual Physiological AssessmentsBody Mass IndexBody mass index (BMI) is a standardized classification tool for indirectly assessing body fat based on a client’s height and weight. BMI classification categories include underweight, healthy weight, overweight, and obese with correlations related to an individual’s risk of heart disease, high blood pressure, Type II diabetes, and other health conditions (National Institute of Health). For this calculation, it is important to input accurate values for height and weight.
To measure height, a client should be instructed to do the following:


    Stand flat with heels, back, buttocks, and shoulders against a wall.
    Keep the head straight and chin level to the ground.
    Mark a line on the wall directly at the top of the head, not including the hair.
    Step away from the wall and then use a tape measure to precisely measure the distance from the ground to the drawn line.

### Content

Chapter 5Administering Virtual AssessmentsLesson 2Virtual Physiological AssessmentsBMI can be determined using several methods, including mathematical formulas available in both metric and imperial units and various online calculators. There are two formulas to calculate BMI. It is calculated by either dividing a person’s weight in kilograms by the square of their height in meters or dividing their body weight in pounds by the square of their height in inches and multiplying by 703.

    Metric Formula: BMI = weight (kg) ÷ [height (m)]2
    Imperial Formula: BMI = 703 x weight (lbs.) ÷ [height (in)]2


To avoid errors in calculation fitness professionals should find a reliable BMI calculator app or online tool, such as the Adult BMI Calculator (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2020), which automatically classifies the client’s BMI. If the client does not have a scale, purchasing one that reports BMI may be encouraged. Similarly, these tools require accurate inputs of height and weight.

To facilitate the communication and monitoring of BMI results, many smart scales offer the ability to manually share the results and trend graphs with a single click. If this resource is not available, fitness professionals should add a column for BMI input into a prepared Google Sheet or other spreadsheet program or provide instructions to e-mail and/or text results immediately following measurement.
Coach's CornerIt is important to note that BMI has limitations in certain populations, including individuals and athletes with higher bone density and muscle mass.

### Waist-to-Hip Ratio

Chapter 5Administering Virtual AssessmentsLesson 2Virtual Physiological AssessmentsWaist-to-Hip RatioMeasuring waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) is another cost-effective and simple way to perform an assessment that provides insight into a client’s body composition and the ability to show progress over time through scheduled reassessment. In a study published by the Annals of Internal Medicine, researchers found that obesity defined by WHR was associated with higher mortality rate than body mass index (Sahakyan et al., 2015).

The only equipment required to assess waist and hip circumference is a measuring tape, which is typically found at local general merchandise, pharmacy, or online retailers. A measuring tape should not be confused with a tape measure, which is more rigid and will not snugly wrap around the body.

To measure waist and hip circumference, it is important to provide guidance or instructional images to the client to ensure these areas are accurately and precisely measured. For initial baseline measurements, anthropometric assessments should be conducted via video conferencing to ensure the appropriate physical landmarks are adhered to. Follow-up assessments may be conducted via phone call or self-administered, but fitness professionals should assess for abnormal values and revert to video conferencing as needed.
To accurately measure waist circumference, do the following:


    Identify the narrowest point of the torso below the rib cage and above the iliac crest (top portion of the hip bone) at the hips.
    Take this measurement as close to the skin as possible.
    Be sure the tape runs horizontally around the entire body circumference.
    Keep the tape flat and avoid any twisting.
    The tape should be pulled snugly but not to the point of causing an indentation in the skin.
    Take the waist measurement at end-tidal volume, following normal expiration.
    Record scores to nearest millimeter or 1/16”.

Chapter 5Administering Virtual AssessmentsLesson 2Virtual Physiological AssessmentsTo accurately measure hip circumference, do the following:

    Stand straight with feet together and locate the widest portion of the hips, often at the level of the mid-buttocks.
    Wrap the measuring tape snugly around this area, ensuring that the measuring tape is level and not angled. The measuring tape should not be pulled so tight as to compress the skin.
    Record the hip measurement.
    Take the measurement at least twice to ensure accuracy. If the values do not match, perform until repeatable measurements are obtained.


To calculate waist-to-hip ratio, simply convert the measurements to centimeters (cm) and divide the waist measurement by the hip measurement. The World Health Organization (WHO, 2008) defines abdominal obesity as a WHR above 0.90 in males and above 0.85 in females (2008) (Figure: Hip Circumference).

### Circumference Measurements

### Bioelectrical Impedance Analysis (BIA)

Chapter 5Administering Virtual AssessmentsLesson 2Virtual Physiological AssessmentsBioelectrical Impedance Analysis (BIA)Bioelectrical Impedance Analysis (BIA) is a reliable measurement tool for assessing additional anthropometric measurements, including fat mass, skeletal muscle mass, total body water, and protein and mineral content. Obtaining these additional measurements will allow fitness professionals to go beyond simple scale weight monitoring to objectively assess changes in body fat and muscle to show meaningful progress and optimize client adherence and retention.

BIA works by passing a small and precise frequency of electricity through the body and measuring the impedance or resistance caused by specific types of body composition. For example, this electricity will flow smoothly through water, but will have higher levels of resistance when passing through fat tissue. This assessment requires physical contact on electrode sites, most commonly both feet (two contact points) when using a scale incorporating BIA technology, but often includes the hands (four contact points) in more advanced devices as well.
Koldunov Alexey/Shutterstock.comFIGURETechnology for BIADepending on the program goal, a fitness professional may consider recommending that their client visit a location that offers BIA testing or purchase an at home BIA scale for regular assessment of body composition. In both instances, the results of single or multiple assessments may be shared as a PDF via e-mail or directly through a reporting software (Figure: Technology for BIA). While BIA is considered a generally safe assessment, it is important to note that there are contraindications, including pacemakers, metal implants, and pregnancy. In addition, BIA assessment should be done under similar circumstances, if possible, as they are easily manipulated by hydration status, proximity to the last workout, and even digestive content.

### Before/After Photos

Chapter 5Administering Virtual AssessmentsLesson 2Virtual Physiological AssessmentsBefore/After PhotosBeyond showing quantitative progress, clients are often reinforced by actually visualizing their progress. Instructing clients to take photos of their body over time is simple and readily obtainable with only the need of a basic camera. However, this should be performed with consideration of consistency in conditions, angles, outfit, and lighting.
Conditions refers to taking body images at the same time and under the same conditions each time. For example, if a client takes baseline images first thing in the morning, after skipping dinner the evening before, but then takes the follow-up images on a full stomach or after drinking a lot of water, these follow up images can lead to a perceived lack of results when meaningful progress has been made. If a client wants to flex for baseline body images, they should be instructed to do the same for all follow-up photos.
Obtaining images at the wrong or inconsistent angle can misrepresent the actual appearance of the human body and limit the ability to effectively show progress. In general, clients should be recommended to use the following angles for body progress photos (Figure: Body Progress Photos Angles):

    Frontal: Directly facing the camera, standing with normal, relaxed posture
    Side: Facing perpendicular to the camera, standing with normal, relaxed posture
    Back: Facing away from the camera, with normal, relaxed posture

### Overhead Squat Assessment

Chapter 5Administering Virtual AssessmentsLesson 3Virtual Movement and Mobility AssessmentsOverhead Squat AssessmentThe overhead squat assessment is a reliable functional evaluation of kinetic chain movement quality, neuromuscular control, and compensatory mechanisms that can be safely performed in a remote capacity with most client types. This assessment should only be performed under the live guidance of a qualified fitness professional using video conferencing or if the instructions are explicit and clear. It may be recorded by the client and sent to the fitness professional for observation and analysis. Fitness professionals should record this assessment for later interpretation and comparison to focus real-time efforts on guiding the client through the process safely and effectively.

While the overhead squat assessment may be used—and useful—for all client types, research has shown that it may provide more information in less-experienced clients and athletes who are still learning to control complex movement patterns (Bishop et al., 2016).
To perform an overhead squat assessment remotely (Figure: Overhead Squat Technique), consider the following:

    The client should ask for help from a friend or set up their camera to ensure stability. The camera should be level, and the client should be framed to capture the top of the fingertips down to a clear visual of both feet and ankles. Excess dead space above or below these physical landmarks should be avoided.
    Ideally, the client should wear form-fitting clothing within their level of comfort that does not match or blend into their background. A solid color and complete background (a wall) is preferable for observation and interpretation.
    Clients should be instructed to remove their socks and shoes for a clear visual of the feet and ankles.
    With the feet positioned shoulder-width apart and feet pointed directly forward, the client should raise both arms completely overhead with the elbows fully extended.
    Instruct the client to maintain arms in this overhead position, to the best of their ability, while squatting to a depth that brings their upper legs parallel to the ground (roughly to the height of a typical chair) or as deeply as possible without significant discomfort for a total of five repetitions.
    The assessment should be repeated with identical instructions from anterior, posterior, and lateral vantage points. To standardize the assessment, the camera should be left in the same place and the client instructed to simply rotate their body in relation to the camera to change angles.

### Single-Leg Squat Assessment

Chapter 5Administering Virtual AssessmentsLesson 3Virtual Movement and Mobility AssessmentsSingle-Leg Squat AssessmentThe single-leg squat (SLS) is a simple, reliable, and relatively easy to administer assessment of stability, strength, balance, and motor control of the lower extremity, and Lumbo-Pelvic-Hip complex regions. Due to the complexity, strength, and balance requirements to perform an SLS assessment, it has been shown that physically active individuals with greater hip and knee flexion seem to be at lower risk to perform the SLS than non-physically active individuals (Gianola et al., 2017). The SLS assessment should be performed at the fitness professional’s discretion based on the physical activity levels and injury history of the client. Similarly to the OHS, this assessment should be conducted during a live video conference and recorded for later interpretation in order to focus real-time efforts on guiding the client through the assessment safely and effectively. Conversely, if the instructions are explicit and clear, it may be recorded by the client and sent to the fitness professional for observation and analysis.
To perform an SLS assessment remotely, Livengood, Dimattia & Uhl (2004) recommends the following (Figure: Single-leg Squat Technique):

    The client should ask for help from a friend or set up their camera with live video conferencing to ensure stability. The camera should be level, and the client should be framed to capture the top of the head down to a clear visual of both feet and ankles. Excess dead space above or below these physical landmarks should be avoided.
    Ideally the client should wear form-fitting clothing, within their level of comfort, that provides visualization of both knees during all portions of the SLS assessment and that does not match or blend into their background. A solid color and complete background (a wall) is preferable for observation and interpretation.
    Clients should be instructed to remove their socks and shoes for a clear visual of the feet and ankles.
    Instruct the client to place their hands on their hips and to stand on a single leg with the other leg lifted off the ground, hip and knee flexed at a 45- and 90-degree angle, respectively. The foot of the non-stance leg should remain directly beside the stance leg/foot without touching it.
    While maintaining hands on hips, the client should squat to approximately 60 degrees of knee flexion or as deeply as possible without losing balance before returning to the start position in less than 6 seconds.
    Perform up to five repetitions before switching sides.
    Repeat identical assessment protocol on the other leg to assess for compensations and differences bilaterally.

### Pushing/Pulling Assessments

Chapter 5Administering Virtual AssessmentsLesson 3Virtual Movement and Mobility AssessmentsPushing/Pulling AssessmentsPushing and pulling assessments are a noninvasive method for assessing movement efficiency, mechanics, and muscle imbalances of the scapulae and shoulders and stability of the lumbo-pelvic-hip complex, cervical spine, and head. These assessments often require access to training equipment, such as free weights, a pulley system, or resistance bands, and may not be applicable or available for all remote-training clients. Ideally, the assessment should be performed live using video conferencing and recorded for later analysis and interpretation in order to focus real-time efforts on guiding the client through a safe and effective assessment. However, if the instructions are explicit and clear, and/or a video example with instructions is provided to the client, it may be a viable option for the client to record themselves performing the movements and send the recording to the fitness professional for observation and analysis. For the purposes of virtual assessments, variations utilizing a resistance exercise band are as follows.
To perform remote pushing and pulling assessments (Figure: Pushing/Pulling Techniques), consider the following:

    The client should ask for help from a friend or set up their camera (with live video conferencing) to ensure stability. The camera should be level, and the client should be framed to capture the top of the head down to a clear visual of both feet and ankles. Excess dead space above or below these physical landmarks should be avoided.
    It is important that the client wear form-fitting clothing within their level of comfort that does not match or blend into their background. A solid color and complete background (a wall) is preferable for interpretation.
    Choose a resistance band that allows for a minimum of 20 repetitions in each assessment. These assessments are not meant to assess muscular strength or cause significant fatigue.
    The client should be instructed to find a stationary object, such as a pole, to anchor the resistance band around ensuring equal length and tension on each side of the band. If the client has a door anchor specifically for use with resistance bands, they can certainly use that placed between the closed door and door jamb. Do not use an object that may move or fall over during this assessment.

### Plank Test

Chapter 5Administering Virtual Assessments Lesson 4Virtual Muscular Endurance AssessmentsPlank TestThe plank test provides insight into a client’s core and upper extremity strength, stability, and endurance. This timed assessment requires a stopwatch, which comes pre-installed on most connected devices and computers in the clock application.
To perform a virtual plank test, consider the following:

    The client should begin in a prone position on a level surface with the elbows and forearms placed on the ground. The elbows should be placed directly under the shoulders, level and in-line with mid-sternum to lower pectorals, with the toes pointing downward toward the ground.
    Instruct the client to begin the stopwatch and then lift the hips until their lower back and pelvis is parallel with the ground. Encourage the client to maintain a neutral head and neck alignment with the eyes focused down toward the floor between the hands.
    Instruct the client to hold this position for as long as possible but stop if pain or severe discomfort arises.
    The timer should be stopped when the client reaches failure, which is defined as inability to keep the hips off the ground, or form and technique are compromised in an attempt to maintain the required position—evidenced by compensations such as consistent arching or slouching of the lower back, excessive rounding or drooping of the upper back, and dropping or extending of the head and neck.

Prostock-studio/Shutterstock.comWhile this assessment is valuable, it may be difficult to standardize the results due to the subjectivity of failure. For this reason, a fitness professional may consider performing the plank test during a recorded video conferencing session for post-analysis to determine the actual duration achieved.

### Wall Sit Test

Chapter 5Administering Virtual Assessments Lesson 4Virtual Muscular Endurance AssessmentsWall Sit TestThe wall sit test is a simple assessment of lower-body strength endurance, primarily of the quadriceps, hamstrings, and glute musculature. This timed assessment requires a stopwatch, which comes pre-installed on most connected devices and computers in the clock application.
To perform a virtual wall sit test, consider the following:

    Instruct the client to locate a flat, non-slippery surface next to a sturdy wall.
    With their back against the wall, have the client slowly slide down into a seated position, moving the feet away from the wall until a comfortable position is found with the knees and hips bent at 90 degrees. Ensure the knees are positioned directly above the feet and the thighs are parallel to the ground. Mark or make note of this position and have them stand back up.
    With the feet shoulder-width apart, instruct the client to move the feet out to this measured or marked distance—keeping the feet flat on the ground. Arms should be crossed on the chest or extended straight out to the front of the body, parallel to the ground.
    Upon achieving this position, start the timer.
    The client should hold this position for as long as possible but stop if pain or severe discomfort arises.
    The timer should be stopped when the client reaches failure, which is defined as an inability to maintain the 90-degree hip and knee angle.

rock-the-stock/Shutterstock.comFitness professionals should use their judgement when determining whether to perform the wall sit test. It may not be suitable for certain individuals, particularly those with a history of knee pain or lower-body injury.
While clients may perform this on their own and self-report results or record their performance for the fitness professional to view later, ideally, this assessment should be performed live using video conferencing and recorded for later analysis and interpretation in order to focus real-time efforts on guiding and motivating the client through the assessment safely and effectively.
